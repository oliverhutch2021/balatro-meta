New to Balatro? The game in a paragraph

Balatro is a solitaire card game built on poker hands. A run is eight rounds, called antes, each with three score targets, called blinds, that roughly double from one to the next; the third of each ante is a boss blind with a rule of its own. You hold eight cards, play up to four poker hands and discard up to three times per blind, and each hand scores chips × mult. Beat ante 8's boss and the run is won. Between blinds a shop sells jokers (up to five held, and the source of nearly all scoring), planet cards that level up a hand type, tarot cards that change your cards, vouchers and packs; money earns interest. Everything random comes from the run's seed. Stakes are difficulty levels, White the easiest; decks change the starting rules. The bot plays White Stake with the Red Deck, the game's first table.

The climb

From 0.07% to 75.6% in a week

Each point is the best version of the bot at that moment, measured on fresh seeds it never trained on. The big steps came from deciding by value, from a network that learns which positions win, from tuning the value's own numbers, from focusing on one hand, from giving the networks more say against the formula, and lastly from thinking harder: three times the samples from ante 5 and four times in boss blinds (+5.3 points on the same 8,000 seeds), then three times the samples everywhere (+3.8 more, p = 4 × 10⁻¹¹). The points marked exam are 8,000 seeds kept apart and never used to choose anything: the 3 November bot scored 66.5% there, the newest 75.6%, the same as on its tuning seeds.

How it plays

A faithful game, a fair player

The engine

Our own copy of Balatro 1.0.1o in Rust: all 150 jokers, 52 consumables, 32 vouchers, 24 tags and 28 boss blinds. The same seed deals the same cards, shops and packs as the real game.

Fair play

The bot sees only what the screen shows. It never sees the seed, the draw order or the next shop. When it looks ahead, it plays on copies whose hidden cards are reshuffled, the way a player imagines what might come.

Deciding by value

Every move, from a shop purchase to the cards in a hand, is tried on copies and judged by one number: the chance of beating each blind ahead, the run's scoring power later, and money as buying power. A network trained on its own runs adds its estimate of the chance of winning.

How we know the game is right

While the viewer runs, a hidden copy of the real game replays the bot's runs move by move and every number on screen is compared with the engine's; a difference stops that run's check and is saved with both states. 78 have been saved since 29 October. Replayed on today's engine, 25 are fixed and 53 open: 23 scores one chip apart (a rounding difference, the next fidelity job), 4 larger scoring gaps, 14 card markings under bosses (debuffs and enhancements), 6 hands dealt after a pack, 4 cash-outs a dollar or two apart and 2 rounds the engine ends that the game doesn't. Fidelity is a standing job, not a finished one.

How it learns

Play, label, learn, repeat

Each round, the best bot so far plays 25,000 new runs. Every position it saw is labelled with how its run ended, and a network learns the chance of winning from any position. The next round plays with that network.

PlayThe best bot plays 25,000 fresh seeds, about 4 million positions.
LabelEach position is marked won or lost by how its run ended.
LearnA network learns the chance of winning from the screen alone, on the GPU in minutes.
Play betterThe bot decides with the network's estimate. Round 4 gained 1.75 points, round 5 only 0.4.
ExploreNew from round 7: one decision in ten takes another option rated nearly as good, so the network sees where untried choices lead.

Without exploration, the network only learns from the choices the bot already makes, so each round can only refine its habits. That is why the gains were shrinking. Rounds 7 to 10 explored, round 10 with whole builds (a quarter of its runs commit from ante 3 to one hand picked at random), and their networks are no better than round 7's: round 10's won 66.1% at four times the formula's weight, where round 8's had won 66.5% at two and a half. More say for the networks collapsed past that point (63% at eight times, 53.8% at sixteen): picking the best of many options with a judge that errs picks its errors. So on 5 November the project stopped tuning the judge and asked, with the two experiments below, where the losses actually come from.

Where it loses · the diagnosis of 5 Nov (the 5 Nov champion; the current one is diagnosed next)

Late, at a boss, and just short

The champion's 3,000 recorded runs, played again move by move: 1,160 were lost, 82% of them at a boss and 70% at antes 5 to 8. They are near misses: 44% scored 80% or more of the target they fell to and another 43% between half and 80%. Only 13% were hopeless. At the loss the median run still held $22 it never spent.

Could better play have beaten the blind?

Every lost blind was played again from its start by the same bot with four times the samples, with the run's own cards (a diagnosis, never the bot's play). It beat 36% of them: 49% of the near misses, 23% at ante 8. By boss, the split between build and play is sharp.

What the experiments found

More search, everywhere: +9.1 points

Forks: are its shop choices wrong?

At 6,750 shop and pack decisions, each of the bot's best options and leaving were played out to the end of the run on fair copies that shared the same hidden futures. Its own choice, its networks' ranking and picking at random all came within a point of win rate of each other, and a network trained on the forks played worse than one trained without them. Choosing between its top options is not where the losses are.

Budget: does it think hard enough?

No. The same bot with three times the samples from ante 6 won 69.4% against 66.4% on the same 8,000 seeds; from ante 5, 70.7% (p = 2 × 10⁻²⁰); with four times the samples in boss blinds as well, 71.8%; with three times the samples at every ante, 75.6% (+3.8 more, p = 4 × 10⁻¹¹), and 75.6% on the exam too. Six times everywhere added about 2 points more on a 2,000-seed screen (79.2% against 77.05%, p = 0.053). The biggest gain of the project came from the judgement it already had, applied harder everywhere, and it carried to every stake: half again as many wins at Red, Green and Black.

What it costs, and the smarter budget

Three times the samples everywhere makes a run about seven times slower than the first teacher's: 8,000 runs take five hours on sixteen cores; six times would double that again. So the budget is being made adaptive: when a decision's two best options are close, those two are judged again with more samples, and every other decision costs nothing extra. Its first screen lost 1.75 points, to a bias (after the two were re-judged, an unchecked third option could win on luck); the fixed version is on the bench now.

Skipping blinds for tags

Made a move judged by value like any other (the skip and the blind each played out on fair copies to where the tag has acted), the bot skipped 0.34 blinds a run, the ones a player takes: an Investment tag at ante 1, edition jokers, Economy when rich. On 2,000 seeds it came out level, 76.85% against 77.05%: at White Stake a blind and its shop are worth about what a tag is. The harder stakes, where the Small Blind pays nothing, are where it should matter, and are next.
