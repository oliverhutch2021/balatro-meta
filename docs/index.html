New to Balatro? The game in a paragraph

Balatro is a solitaire card game built on poker hands. A run is eight rounds, called antes, each with three score targets, called blinds, that roughly double from one to the next; the third of each ante is a boss blind with a rule of its own. You hold eight cards, play up to four poker hands and discard up to three times per blind, and each hand scores chips × mult. Beat ante 8's boss and the run is won. Between blinds a shop sells jokers (up to five held, and the source of nearly all scoring), planet cards that level up a hand type, tarot cards that change your cards, vouchers and packs; money earns interest. Everything random comes from the run's seed. Stakes are difficulty levels, White the easiest; decks change the starting rules. The bot plays White Stake with the Red Deck, the game's first table.

Move over the table to steer the felt; tap to stir it.

The climb

From 0.07% to 71.8% in a week

Each point is the best version of the bot at that moment, measured on fresh seeds it never trained on. The big steps came from deciding by value, from a network that learns which positions win, from tuning the value's own numbers, from focusing on one hand, from giving the networks more say against the formula, and on 5 November from thinking harder where runs are decided: three times the samples from ante 5 and four times in boss blinds, +5.3 points on the same 8,000 seeds. The point marked exam is 8,000 seeds kept apart and never used to choose anything; the newest bot hasn't sat it yet.

How it plays

A faithful game, a fair player

The engine

Our own copy of Balatro 1.0.1o in Rust: all 150 jokers, 52 consumables, 32 vouchers, 24 tags and 28 boss blinds. The same seed deals the same cards, shops and packs as the real game. While the viewer runs, a hidden copy of the real game replays the bot's runs move by move and every number is compared; a difference is saved with both states. Sixty have been saved since 29 October; replayed on today's engine, 25 are fixed and 35 open, most of them scores one chip apart (a rounding difference under study), the rest card debuffs under bosses, hands dealt after a pack and a few larger scoring gaps. Fidelity is a standing job, not a finished one.

Fair play

The bot sees only what the screen shows. It never sees the seed, the draw order or the next shop. When it looks ahead, it plays on copies whose hidden cards are reshuffled, the way a player imagines what might come.

Deciding by value

Every move, from a shop purchase to the cards in a hand, is tried on copies and judged by one number: the chance of beating each blind ahead, the run's scoring power later, and money as buying power. A network trained on its own runs adds its estimate of the chance of winning.

How it learns

Play, label, learn, repeat

Each round, the best bot so far plays 25,000 new runs. Every position it saw is labelled with how its run ended, and a network learns the chance of winning from any position. The next round plays with that network.

PlayThe best bot plays 25,000 fresh seeds, about 4 million positions.
LabelEach position is marked won or lost by how its run ended.
LearnA network learns the chance of winning from the screen alone, on the GPU in minutes.
Play betterThe bot decides with the network's estimate. Round 4 gained 1.75 points, round 5 only 0.4.
ExploreNew from round 7: one decision in ten takes another option rated nearly as good, so the network sees where untried choices lead.

Without exploration, the network only learns from the choices the bot already makes, so each round can only refine its habits. That is why the gains were shrinking. Rounds 7 to 10 explored, round 10 with whole builds (a quarter of its runs commit from ante 3 to one hand picked at random), and their networks are no better than round 7's: round 10's won 66.1% at four times the formula's weight, where round 8's had won 66.5% at two and a half. More say for the networks collapsed past that point (63% at eight times, 53.8% at sixteen): picking the best of many options with a judge that errs picks its errors. So on 5 November the project stopped tuning the judge and asked, with the two experiments below, where the losses actually come from.

Where it loses · the diagnosis of 5 Nov

Late, at a boss, and just short

The champion's 3,000 recorded runs, played again move by move: 1,160 were lost, 82% of them at a boss and 70% at antes 5 to 8. They are near misses: 44% scored 80% or more of the target they fell to and another 43% between half and 80%. Only 13% were hopeless. At the loss the median run still held $22 it never spent.

Could better play have beaten the blind?

Every lost blind was played again from its start by the same bot with four times the samples, with the run's own cards (a diagnosis, never the bot's play). It beat 36% of them: 49% of the near misses, 23% at ante 8. By boss, the split between build and play is sharp.

The build gap compounds from ante 4

Runs that go on to lose at antes 5 to 8 are not undone by one bad decision. Their biggest hand trails the winners' by 11% at ante 4, 19% at 5, 24% at 6 and 34% at 7, and they are poorer at every ante: a weaker economy feeding a weaker engine from ante 3 on.

What the two experiments found

More search where it matters: +5.3 points

Forks: are its shop choices wrong?

At 6,750 shop and pack decisions, each of the bot's best options and leaving were played out to the end of the run on fair copies that shared the same hidden futures. Its own choice, its networks' ranking and picking at random all came within a point of win rate of each other, and a network trained on the forks played worse than one trained without them. Choosing between its top options is not where the losses are.

Budget: does it think hard enough?

The same bot with three times the samples from ante 6 won 69.4% against 66.4% on the same 8,000 seeds; from ante 5, 70.7% (p = 2 × 10⁻²⁰); with four times the samples in boss blinds as well, 71.8%. The biggest gain of the project, from the judgement it already had, applied harder where runs are decided.

What it costs

About four times the time per run: 8,000 runs take three and a half hours on sixteen cores instead of forty minutes. Fine for a bot, slower for trials, which now screen on 2,000 seeds and confirm on 8,000. The open question is how far the budget scales: from ante 3, from ante 1, six times instead of three.

The human teacher

A human player's lessons, tested

The project's human player wins about three runs in four at White Stake and records those runs move by move, so the bot's choice can be set against the player's at every decision. The player's losses come early (antes 1 to 3); the bot's come late. Each lesson below became an option and was tested on the same 2,000 seeds with and without it.

Focus on one hand, tested with fairer pack skipping on 8,000 seeds, gained 1.64 points (p = 0.001) and is now how the bot plays. None of the other gains is clear on 2,000 seeds, and the rules added as extra points (x mult, a thinner deck) made the bot worse: they skew everything else it weighs. The lessons that help will have to be found by the bot itself, which is what exploration is for. The player's runs show what it is looking for: on 1 November one took Straight from level 1 to 19 in one run and Flush to level 33 in another, where the bot's main hand ends its wins at a median level 5.

The harder tables

Every stake, once

Stakes add their rules one by one: Red takes a discard, Green makes the targets grow faster, Black lets jokers be eternal, Blue takes a hand, Purple makes the targets grow faster again, Orange adds perishable jokers, Gold adds rentals. Networks that learned every stake's runs (two nights, 4 November) played worse than the White champion at every stake but Gold, and a correction trained on where the champion is wrong at each stake predicted far better and played worse: both were a better-calibrated judge the bot then trusted too much.
